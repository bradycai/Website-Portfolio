## The problem

A small brand selling on Shopify, TikTok Shop, Amazon, and eBay gets its orders as four separate streams of notification emails. Nothing adds up stock across them, so the same hoodie can sell out on three storefronts at once before anyone notices.

## What it does

OrderSync pulls every order into one queue, keeps a running count of committed stock, and keeps an **action center** for anything about to go wrong: stock shortages, overdue shipments, and products it can't match with confidence.

Routine warnings, like an overdue shipment that only needs a follow-up, can be prepared in one click. Critical issues, shortages, and uncertain matches always stay with the seller. For those, the seller picks a resolution (a delay, a partial shipment, a refund, or a stock correction) and Claude drafts the customer message.

## How it's built

- **AI for judgment, never arithmetic.** Claude reads pasted emails, suggests SKU matches, and drafts messages. Stock math, duplicate detection, deadlines, and alert thresholds are plain TypeScript in a shared package that the server and UI both use.
- **Imports are idempotent.** An order's identity is its channel plus its marketplace order ID, enforced by a unique index. A repeated notification or a second sync updates the existing order instead of creating a copy.
- **Uncertain matches stay out of the math.** A SKU suggestion under 90% confidence reserves no stock until the seller confirms it, and the confirmed mapping is remembered for next time.
- **Stock is never double-counted.** Starting stock is physical stock, changed only by an approved correction. Commitments are recomputed from the order list on every request.
- **An unstated field is not an assertion.** Most notification emails say nothing about fulfilment, so a re-import leaves the stored status alone. Otherwise a resent email could move a shipped order back to unshipped and re-commit stock that already left.

## After the hackathon

The hackathon build ran on SQLite. Afterwards I rebuilt it to be production-shaped:

- **PostgreSQL with numbered migrations** and one repository module per table. Multi-step writes run in transactions.
- **Constraints beat checks.** Rules that must survive two servers racing, such as order identity and one automated follow-up per alert, live in the schema as unique indexes rather than in application code.
- **Stateless instances.** Sessions and sync leases live in the database, so any number of servers can run side by side. It ships as a Docker image.
- **Accounts and roles.** scrypt-hashed passwords, owner, admin, and staff roles checked on the server, and an audit log of who changed what.
- **A sync engine with a seam.** The engine owns scheduling, cursors, leases, retries, and dedupe. A marketplace only has to implement a small connector interface to fetch and translate orders.
- **A dashboard built around real numbers.** Revenue, order value, on-time rate, and inventory value are computed in SQL, with per-channel charts and a dark interface.

## What's real and what isn't

The data layer, auth, roles, and sync pipeline are real. The marketplace connectors are sample feeds: connecting to Shopify or Amazon needs a registered developer app and per-seller tokens, so rather than fake that, the connector is the one piece left as a seam. Customer emails and stock updates are simulated and labelled that way in the UI.

Without an API key, the app runs in a demo mode with prepared AI outputs, and that's how these screenshots were taken.
